# How to Fill a Mission Trip Team

Most trips do not stall on logistics. They stall because the people who would have said yes were never actually asked. This is how to do the asking, and how to keep going when it is slow.

You had the idea. You prayed about it, you found a partner on the ground, you picked the dates. Then you stood up in front of the church, announced the trip, and waited.

Two people signed up. Both of them are already on your leadership team.

It is very easy, in that quiet, to conclude something crushing: that your church does not care about missions. Leaders quit over this. Not over visas or budgets, over the silence that follows an announcement. So start here, because it is almost always true:

> Silence is not a no. It is usually the sound of nobody having been asked.

An announcement is an invitation addressed to nobody in particular. Everyone hearing it assumes it is meant for someone else, someone with more time, more money, more faith, or a passport. The announcement is not wasted, it makes the trip real and public. It is just not an ask, and it was never going to be one.

## Replace the announcement with a list of names

Before you promote anything, sit down and write fifteen to twenty names. Not "the youth group" or "young families". Names.

Then ask them one at a time, in person or by voice, and tell each one why it was their name you wrote down. "I thought of you because you are unflappable with teenagers." "Because you speak Spanish and you never mention it." "Because you have been asking good questions about what our church is actually for."

That sentence is the whole difference. A group text asks people to volunteer. A named reason tells someone they were seen, and it is much harder to set down.

Expect to ask twice

Almost nobody says yes the first time, and most people do not say no either. They say "let me think about it" and then life closes over it. Plan the second ask into your calendar when you make the first one. The second ask is not pestering, it is the one that gets answered.

## Give them a date to answer by

An application with no closing date gets deferred forever, by people who fully intend to fill it in. A deadline is not pressure, it is a kindness: it converts a vague good intention into a decision someone can actually make.

Set it early, say it every time you mention the trip, and hold it. Then do the thing most leaders forget: **respond fast**. An applicant who hears nothing for a month concludes the trip is not really happening, and tells their friends the same. Confirm people within a week, even if the answer is "we have you, paperwork comes in March."

## Make the first step smaller than the trip

Asking someone to commit ten days and two thousand dollars in a single conversation is asking them to decide everything at once. Most people cannot, so they decide nothing.

Stage it. The first ask is not "come on the trip", it is "come hear about it". An information night, a coffee, a fifteen minute conversation after the service. Small enough to say yes to without rearranging a life, and once someone is in the room, the trip stops being abstract.

## Ask the already willing first

Go first to the people who went last time, the ones who ask about the trip unprompted, the ones who gave money to someone else's support letter. Your first three yeses are not just three seats filled. They are three people who will recruit for you, in conversations you will never be part of, with a credibility you do not have about your own trip.

Ask each of them the same follow-up question: _who else should I be asking?_ That question has filled more teams than any flyer ever has.

## Money is the silent no

The most common unspoken reason people do not respond is that they cannot picture affording it, and they are too embarrassed to say so. If you name the cost and stop there, the number is a closed door.

Say the cost and how it gets covered in the same breath. That most participants raise most of it through support letters. That people who have never raised a dollar in their lives regularly get fully funded. That you will sit with them and write the first letter together. Cost is not the obstacle nearly as often as _not knowing support raising is normal_ is the obstacle.

If you need the practical version, the [fundraising ideas guide](https://missiontripplanning.com/mission-trip-fundraising-ideas) covers what actually works, and the [budget guide](https://missiontripplanning.com/budget) shows where the money goes.

## A ninety day rhythm

Recruiting fails when it is one push. It works when it is a rhythm, because the same person hears it at three different moments in their own life and one of those moments is the right one.

Ninety days from first vision to closed applications

Counted back from the application deadline, not from departure. Compress it if you must, but keep the shape: a public cast, then private asks, then a low-commitment room, then a decision point.

1.  90

    90 days out

    Write your list of names. Cast the vision publicly once, so the trip is real and people have heard of it before you call them.

2.  75

    75 days out

    First wave of direct asks. Start with the already willing, and ask every one of them who else you should be asking.

3.  60

    60 days out

    Information night. Low commitment, high specificity: the partner, the work, the dates, the cost, and how the cost gets covered.

4.  45

    45 days out

    Applications open with the deadline announced. Share the link everywhere the trip has already been mentioned.

5.  30

    30 days out

    Second ask to everyone who said "let me think about it". This is the conversation that fills the team.

6.  0

    Deadline

    Close it, and confirm everyone within a week. Fast answers are what make the next trip easier to fill.


## When it still does not fill

Sometimes you do all of it and you get six people instead of twelve. Hear this clearly: a team of six who are actually called is not a failed trip. It is a smaller trip, and smaller trips are often better ones. Fewer people to house, more room for each of them to be changed, and a partner who is not overwhelmed by a crowd.

The burnout in mission leadership rarely comes from the work. It comes from carrying an outcome that was never yours to carry. You are responsible for the invitation, for making it clear, personal and honest. You are not responsible for the answer. Somebody else is doing that work, in rooms you will never be in.

> The beauty of following God comes with the first step. The consistency comes after you realize the power of the first yes. Robert Krause, Founder

Every person you ask is being invited into their own first yes. Some of them have been waiting a long time for someone to say their name out loud. That is true whether the yes is to a plane ticket or to following Jesus at all, which is why [the two halves of this work belong together](https://startdiscipling.com/our-mission).
